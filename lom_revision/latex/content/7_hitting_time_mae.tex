\section{Duration-to-Target: Hitting-Time MAE Analysis}
\label{sec:hitting_time}
\addresses{T1.b, T2.e, AE (``demonstrate earlier stopping'')}

{\color{green!50!black}

\subsection{Motivation}

The cross-dataset accuracy results (Section~\ref{subsec:cross_dataset})
show that \nbmodel regression produces accurate point predictions of
$\Utrue$.
However, the AE and R2 specifically requested evidence that these
predictions \emph{change decisions}.
The most natural decision is experiment-duration planning: given pilot
data, how many additional days are needed to reach a target sample size?

This section translates the forecasting accuracy into a concrete
decision-level metric: the mean absolute error (MAE) in predicted
hitting time, measured in days.

\subsection{Setup}

For each experiment and each participation multiplier
$\eta \in \{1.5, 2.0, 3.0\}$, define the target
\[
    M_\eta = \lceil \eta \cdot N_{\pilotdays} \rceil.
\]
The \emph{true hitting time} $D_\eta$ is the first day $d > \pilotdays$
at which the cumulative number of distinct users reaches $M_\eta$:
\[
    D_\eta = \min\bigl\{d > \pilotdays : \text{cum.\ users at day } d \geq M_\eta\bigr\}.
\]
If the target is not reached within the observation window, $D_\eta$
is censored.

For each fitted method, the \emph{predicted hitting time} $\hat D_\eta$
is obtained by inverting the mean prediction trajectory:
\[
    \hat D_\eta = \min\bigl\{\ell \geq 1 :
    N_{\pilotdays} + \hat U_{\pilotdays}^{(\ell)} \geq M_\eta\bigr\}
    + \pilotdays.
\]
The decision-level error is $|\hat D_\eta - D_\eta|$, measured in days.

\paragraph{Methods compared.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{\nbmodel (regression):} 4-parameter model
        $(\beta, \sigma, c, r)$; mean trajectory via
        \texttt{mean\_number\_new\_users()}.
  \item \textbf{\tgmodel:} 3-parameter model
        $(\beta, \sigma, c)$; mean trajectory via \texttt{GD.mean()}.
  \item \textbf{IBP:} 3-parameter model
        $(\alpha, c, \sigma)$; mean trajectory via \texttt{IBP.mean()}.
  \item \textbf{Linear extrapolation:} assumes constant daily
        arrival rate $N_{\pilotdays}/\pilotdays$; always returns a
        finite prediction.
  \item \textbf{Jackknife (J3):} order-3 jackknife estimator
        applied to the pilot site frequency spectrum.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Censoring.}
Experiments where the true hitting time exceeds the observation
window are excluded from the MAE calculation for that $\eta$ value.
The number of censored experiments is reported alongside MAE.
For linear extrapolation (which always returns a finite prediction),
values exceeding the window are capped at $\pilotdays + \followupdays$.

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
On REES46, all methods perform well at low $\eta$ (the target is
reached quickly, so there is little room for error).
Differences emerge at $\eta = 3$, where the target requires
substantial growth beyond the pilot.
At $k = 100$, \nbmodel regression is off by 2.4~days on average,
vs.\ 2.7~days for linear extrapolation and 2.8~days for IBP.
The advantage is modest because REES46 has very large pilot samples
($\sim$1M users), which provide strong signal for all methods.

\paragraph{Censoring at $\eta = 3$, $k = 21$.}
At $\eta = 3$ with $k = 21$, 11 of 186 experiments are censored
(the target is not reached within 28 days).
\nbmodel regression additionally censors 9 experiments where its
predicted trajectory does not reach the target, vs.\ 2 for \tgmodel
and 0 for linear extrapolation.
This reflects the conservative nature of the \nbmodel mean trajectory
at short horizons.

The larger advantage on ASOS reflects the harder prediction task:
long extrapolation horizons (median $\followupdays/\pilotdays = 7.9$)
and volatile fashion-retail engagement patterns amplify the benefit
of the \nbmodel model's richer parametric structure.

\paragraph{Censoring.}
At $\eta = 3.0$, 76 of 144 arms are censored (the target is not
reached within the observation window).
This is expected: many ASOS experiments have short follow-up periods
relative to the ambitious $3\times$ target.
Among the 68 non-censored arms, \nbmodel regression censors 4
predictions, vs.\ 2 for \tgmodel and 0 for linear extrapolation.

\subsection{Connection to Statistical Power}
\label{subsec:power_connection}

The participation target $M_\eta$ corresponds to a specific
statistical power level for a fixed effect size $\delta$ and
variance $\sigma^2$.
Specifically, for a two-sample $z$-test with equal allocation,
\[
    \text{power} \approx \Phi\!\left(
    \frac{\sqrt{M_\eta/2} \cdot \delta}{\sigma}
    - z_{\alpha/2}\right).
\]
Thus ``time to $M_\eta$ users'' is equivalent to ``time to target
power'' under a fixed $\delta/\sigma$ assumption.
An experimenter who specifies a minimum detectable effect and
desired power can compute the required $M$, then use the hitting-time
prediction to plan the experiment duration.

This connection means that the MAE results above directly quantify
the accuracy of \emph{power-aware duration planning}: \nbmodel
regression predicts the time to reach target power within 2.4~days
(REES46 $k = 100$, $\eta = 3$) or 8.5~days (ASOS, $\eta = 3$),
compared to 2.7--2.8 and 14.3--18.7~days for competitors.


\subsection{Summary and Implications for the Paper}

\paragraph{Key findings.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{ASOS:} \nbmodel regression reduces hitting-time MAE
        by 22--42\% relative to the next-best method.
        This is the strongest result and should be the lead example
        in the paper.
  \item \textbf{REES46 $k = 100$:} \nbmodel regression wins at all
        $\eta$ levels, with a modest but consistent advantage.
        This is the public-data confirmation.
  \item \textbf{UCI:} Consistent with the above but too small for
        strong conclusions.
        Best placed in the supplement.
  \item \textbf{Linear extrapolation is a strong baseline} at short
        horizons but degrades at $\eta = 3$ on ASOS, where the
        power-law slowdown matters most.
\end{enumerate}
